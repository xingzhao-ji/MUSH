# The country beneath the Kindly Crown

Status: **proposal, not built**. This is the setting for the finite six-chapter story in [PLOT.md](PLOT.md). [story_manifest.json](story_manifest.json) fixes the cast, names and quest IDs. [WORLD_PLAN.md](../WORLD_PLAN.md) remains the landscape proposal; this document gives its people a shared history without claiming that its settlements or journeys exist in the current game.

## A small home in an immense place

The player is the existing small cream woodland creature, with rounded ears and a tucked round tail. Their home has warm oval windows, a mushroom roof, a garden and somewhere to leave muddy feet. From its lantern-lit terrace, a path bends through flowers toward a bridge. Beyond the rail, mist fills a valley so broad that the next bench of granite appears to be an island. Waterfalls descend between those benches. Farther away, lit spire houses gather around a colossal mushroom tree.

That tree is **the Kindly Crown, 暖冠**. Its violet-blue cap catches the distance light; its branching underside holds a broad, steady gold. Condensation collects along the gills and falls in bright drops. These drops are water catching the light, not a dwindling store of power. The tree is healthy throughout the story. It does not demand offerings, select a saviour or grow dim to count the player's mistakes. At home, one oval window offers the same kind of welcome on a much smaller scale.

The concept's grandeur comes from three readable distances: a creature-sized chair and garden close enough to touch; a bridge, waterfall and inhabited granite bench across the middle; the Crown and a succession of softer fungal silhouettes beyond. The apparent cliff islands are parts of a connected valley landform, separated in the view by ravines and mist, not a new system of floating terrain. Paths meet safe overlooks; a sweeping view never means that a player must jump a chasm or master Float. The colossal tree is not an ordinary harvestable mushroom scaled up. It is an old, singular living landmark, with light that follows its own gentle biology.

The town at its foot is **Lamploom, 灯织镇**. Its name comes from the crossing rows of windows seen from the home terrace: small warm marks woven between the dark supports of bridges and roots. It is a town made of households, workbenches, sheltered landings and shared places to stop. Its tallest roof is still tiny beneath the Crown. Nobody is required to become important to deserve a window here.

## Water makes the map

High Ice Basin sends meltwater down the West Wall and Falls into Granite Falls Valley. Plunge basins collect the falls, then feed the valley lake and its outlet. The outlet enters Autumn Gorge Woods and ultimately the reed channels and limestone lagoons of Karst Lagoon Coast. Mineral Springs Terraces contribute a warm tributary, with separate dry paths and runoff channels. Tuff Tower Highlands are a drier neighbouring catchment joined by passes and spring-fed terraces; people carry news between those routes rather than pretending all seven regions lie along one river. Basalt Shore has its own cold glacial outwash and connects to the lagoon settlements by coastal exchange.

The shared watershed is therefore a network of sources, tributaries, gorges and coastal journeys. A message that is useful at the ice cannot tell a lagoon ferry how its local landing looks. Every notice needs room for the receiving community's reply. This is both practical water knowledge and the central social habit of the setting.

“Kingdoms” is an old storybook word for these communities. It does not imply competing crowns, borders to conquer or a throne quest. Most decisions in the story happen at a household, a workbench or a public notice stand. Residents belong to places through their work and relationships. Species is not nationality: an otter can grow up in the highlands, and a moth can make a home by the sea. No region has one temperament, one body type or one approved way to live.

## The seven regional cultures

All customs, patterns, building details and terms below are invented from this world's materials and needs. Landscape references in WORLD_PLAN are geological references only. Do not attach the sacred signs, dress, ceremonies, accents or architectural motifs of a real living culture to these peoples. A weather hood is a practical hood, not a borrowed ceremonial silhouette. The art language uses original seams, visible repairs, overlapping leaves, water-level notches and plain geometric joinery.

### High Ice Basin: those who leave the second message

The basin communities live in low dry-stone halls tucked into granite benches. Corbelled roofs, turf coverings and wind walls keep useful space close to the ground. A tool is judged by whether a small pair of paws can use it with a warm wrap on. People carry soft felt sleeves around stone notice tablets so the tablets do not rattle against one another on a walk. Cairns are ordinary waymarks maintained by route keepers, with no sacred meaning or borrowed ritual shape.

The **first melt** is a recurring local gathering when the falls begin their fuller warm-season voice. Households listen from a sheltered place, compare water marks and share thin pieces of cold-cured fungus. The first notice records what the source is doing. The second message records who has heard it and what is happening farther down. A child copying a notice leaves a blank space on purpose, so someone else can answer. Taking time to check a report is a kindness, not a failure of courage.

Avren brings this care to Lamploom, but also brings a personal fear of telling a story incompletely. Other basin residents might be carefree cooks or untidy builders. The shared craft is attention to routes, not a national personality. Valley silk keeps their satchels light; coastal salt preserves food; their notices help people choose comfortable journeys. A future visit could follow Avren to a hall where old and new melt notices sit together on a dry shelf. That is an invitation to a later region, not an unresolved task in this story.

### Granite Falls Valley: those who make room at the water

Granite Falls Valley is the fungal heartland, mild and flowering beneath sheer walls. Timber and mycelium-felt lodges stand on stone plinths above known high-water lines. Mushroom homes have deeply set oval windows. The lowest terrace is for looking and listening; domestic rooms sit farther up. Shelter can be handsome without pretending that spray, wet stone or steep ground has stopped being real.

At the plunge pools, two neighbours traditionally compare what each noticed before either writes a final account. Old flood marks pair a water height with the record of a message and its reply. The custom is called **leaving the other half**. It does not mean every neighbour must agree. It means a description remains open to information from someone standing elsewhere. Shared benches face both the water and one another, so a quiet watcher can belong without speaking to a gathering.

Lamploom grows from the same habit. Shared cupboards keep plain cups and dry wraps. A window niche may be left unassigned for a visitor's small arrangement. A household's garden is a personal patch of welcome, never a ranking against the town's display. Brindle, Thimble, Rillik, Ombel and Quillip each put this inheritance to different uses; Plink and Nuzzle make the larger neighbours' idea of “enough room” pleasantly inadequate. The player helps these neighbours without becoming the owner of their lives.

Spore-silk balloons travel when local conditions suit them, traditionally during calm dawn air or favourable evening flow along warm granite. In the proposed story they are beautiful optional sights. Grounded baskets, shade, warm windows and flowers make an equally complete celebration. The valley sends felt, silk work, garden stories and copied notices outward. It receives salt, dyes, broad cups, route cards and new ways of asking whether a guest is comfortable.

### Mineral Springs Terraces: those who test the place an object rests

Cream and ochre mineral shelves step between turquoise pools. Warm ground meets cool air, leaving restrained steam and frost on farther rims. Settlements occupy stable dry ledges; walked routes and craft tables are distinct from hot pools and runoff. The story does not ask a player to wade into geothermal water or collect from a delicate rim.

Potters make broad-based cups, shallow warming dishes and fitted lids using ordinary prepared clay. Tables have removable test boards: one even, one gently sloped, one textured like a travelling tray. Before a gift leaves the workbench, its maker asks where it will stand. The customary **second setting** is to try it in a different place with someone else. A repair line or a widened foot remains visible as part of the object's history. There is no ideal finish that makes its owner a better guest.

Pumel has made a lovely narrow cup that is less comfortable on Lamploom's display ledge. Helping try a broad base turns an awkward object into a useful question about where it rests and whom it fits. Terrace people trade durable vessels and warm-ground grown food; they receive valley fibres for handles and highland shade cloth. A later visit could show a communal test table with cups made for many kinds of paws. It is a visit to learn from a living place, not an order to fix its culture.

### Autumn Gorge Woods: those who name the resting places

A river narrows between slate walls beneath rust-red and golden leaves. Rain travels along branches before reaching the ground. Homes and small storehouses sit on broad shelves, connected by covered walks where the land permits. Bark-toned roof boards overlap with clearly invented joints; drainage lips guide water away from the path. Dry wraps and route cards hang in ordinary open racks.

People describe a route by its places to rest as well as by its destination. “Two turns and a bench” is more useful than “not far.” The **dry-copy exchange** leaves a readable duplicate of a route card under the next roof, while the first remains where a returning walker expects it. A name on a card identifies its writer, not a territorial claim. Cards are revised after rain; crossed-out lines mean someone learned more.

Copperleaf's attention to comfortable routes is personal, and includes a wry delight in finding a good bench. The whole region is not fearful. Some neighbours relish wet walks; others prefer a long chat under a roof. Their practical agreement is that a shorter journey is not automatically the better one. They trade dried woodland food, stitched covers and route knowledge for salt, felt and cups. A later gorge visit can follow the chain of covered stops upstream or down, with the option to turn back at any rest.

### Tuff Tower Highlands: those who keep the patch visible

Peach, rose and chalk towers rise from a dry landscape. Springs are sparse, so households keep sheltered cisterns and display clear marks for shared water use. Homes cluster on stable benches around work courts; no narrative reward asks for climbing an eroding tower. Shade cloth is practical architecture here, producing cool intervals between sunlit walks.

Spore-silk makers and balloon crews use a **patch record**: a visible piece of contrasting cloth identifies a repair so the next worker knows where to inspect. Matching a patch perfectly is an aesthetic choice, never the price of being accepted. Mending tables keep two small samples, the original fabric and the addition, so both makers' work can be remembered. Reused silk can become a lantern cover, a pocket or an awning after its travelling life ends.

Selvie learns to like the conversation inside a visible repair instead of hiding the join. Puffrel initially treats the balloon's ascent as the complete story and learns to value its quiet ground display. These are two individuals interpreting the same craft differently. Weather notices here describe a choice, not a promise that the sky will obey. People trade shade cloth, ribbon and skilled silk work for valley food and coastal dyes. Future visitors might compare the colour of towers from a shaded court or watch a crew lay out a balloon on the ground. Neither activity is a missing chapter of the main arc.

### Karst Lagoon Coast: those who keep both addresses

Jade lagoons and pale limestone towers open into tidal channels. Woven causeways join stilted halls where the ground and local water knowledge permit. Reed screens can be lifted for air or lowered against rain. Ferry exchanges have covered sorting shelves, with a clear place for things staying and a different place for things travelling. Markets trade valley spore-silk for salt and dyes, and send news inland with the same care as goods.

A shared object may wear **two address tags**. One says where it is useful now; the other says where someone will welcome it back. This avoids pretending that lending, moving or growing fond of a new place cancels the old connection. People read tags aloud together when possible so an unclear place name can be corrected. The practice grew out of ferries serving more than one lagoon, not from a mystical claim over possessions.

Nacri's parcel carries both home and host labels. The player's answer preserves both. Coastal residents have many opinions about travel; Nacri's enthusiasm is not a mandatory trait. Their hospitality offers a dry shelf before asking for a story. An optional future visit can follow a market parcel to its other address, with no collection checklist and no debt to settle.

### Basalt Shore: those who carry a little quiet

Black sand, columnar rock, moss-green hollows and steel-blue water make a colder coast. Braided glacial rivers spread into the sea. Low halls stand behind wind walls; entrances turn away from the common gust. Felt liners soften benches, cups and the small boxes travellers bring inland. Rock is structural and practical, without borrowed carved symbols or sacred alignments.

The **quiet place** is a household courtesy: one seat has less wind, less clatter and no expectation of conversation. When someone visits, a host can offer that seat before offering entertainment. Sea stories may be spoken, drawn in a route book or left until another day. Nobody must prove they enjoyed a gathering by staying in its busiest part.

Brumlet misses the familiar size of the shore's sound. A sheltered landing in Lamploom will not become the sea, but it can become somewhere to remember it comfortably. Shore traders bring salt, durable stone tools and well-lined containers, returning with silk, garden keepsakes and dry food. A later coastal visit might follow the sheltered side of a wind wall to a view of the outwash. It need not become a storm ordeal.

## A history of adding the reply

The oldest everyday story says the Kindly Crown was already broad enough to shade a gathering when the first household set a table on its plateau. No living character knows its exact age. Nobody mistakes this household story for a scientific dating of the tree. The first houses used stone benches already clear of the higher water marks. As households multiplied, their windows could be seen from the opposite terrace, and the place became Lamploom.

The **high-water years** taught the communities to copy useful observations without assuming they applied everywhere. There was inconvenience, wet storage and a great deal of moving baskets, not a lost civilisation or buried horror. People raised store floors, made better dry routes and kept marks visible. An upstream message began to travel with a space for a downstream reply. At the plunge pool, the paired marks preserve an early example: the water arrived, a watcher described it, and another household answered from below.

The **bridge years** joined existing benches with resting landings and made the town's window lines visible as one place. Craft exchange widened. Later, spore-silk balloons offered another way to carry light and news when the air was suitable. The lantern-and-balloon gathering grew from leaving lamps for visitors who arrived at different points in a journey. Its original virtue was patience.

This year's small confusion comes from an **out-of-date gathering notice** copied forward from a previous arrangement. Its wording suggests one shared ascent. The current upstream notice and downstream reply instead describe different comfortable travel windows and a sheltered welcome. The public copy lost that distinction through ordinary reuse, not sabotage. Several neighbours assume someone else has already included the reply. Nobody is secretly a villain, and nobody has caused the water, weather or Crown to fail.

The player makes the overlooked relationship visible. A home with one chosen furnishing can welcome a visitor. Three garden places can help a neighbour recognise a terrace. Two marks can tell a fuller story than one. A steady signal can invite a reply. A notice can name more than one comfortable arrival. The town restores its own patient custom through these small acts.

## The lanterns stay

The proposed finale is a **rolling lantern-and-balloon gathering**. It begins only when the player explicitly chooses to begin. It stays available afterward; arriving later, leaving, returning, choosing rain or selecting night does not spoil it. The scene can include flowers, steady covered lanterns, patched silk, grounded baskets, cups on broad feet and an empty seat. In suitable calm conditions, an optional view may show balloons rising. The sheltered ground celebration tells the complete ending in every weather state.

No time of day is more deserving. In day, the silk colours and garden are clear; at night, the warm windows and lanterns carry the view. Rain moves the welcome under cover and makes reflections visible. Clear weather opens a wider outlook. Dawn and dusk remain possible later authored flavour, not claims that the current source has a clock or automatic scheduled events. Shared motion and reduced-motion preferences should change presentation, never whether a guest may attend.

The final image returns to the first home terrace. It is not larger, richer or fully furnished by decree. It is recognisable as a place the player chose, with unfinished personal space still available. Across the valley, one small light is answered by many. The Crown was healthy before the player arrived and remains healthy when they go indoors.

## Boundaries for later authors

The proposed cultures provide reasons for making, visiting and listening. They do not create a new combat loop, faction score, collectible currency, survival meter, farming delay or mandatory journey. Magic remains the prototype's automatically regenerating resource; planting costs 25 and blooms immediately. Eight garden places and six furniture places remain maxima, not moral tests or festival entry requirements. Keepsakes in the dialogue contract do not unlock new seed, furniture, ability or save-system behaviour.

Walking, looking, Float, Glow, contextual interaction, planting and home decoration are existing verbs. Named people, conversation, quest tracking, give/carry, the town, the Crown and the later region visits are proposed content or systems. A character's invitation never proves a route has been built. Existing runtime and verification documents retain their own evidence and limitations; this story proposal adds no native, exported, headless, motion, audio or device acceptance claim.
